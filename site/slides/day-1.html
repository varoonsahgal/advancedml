The hook

On tables, a 25-year-old idea still beats most deep nets.

Friedman's gradient boosting (2001) vs. modern deep learning on typical tabular benchmarks: Grinsztajn et al., NeurIPS 2022. Today we make it sharp, honest and explainable.

01

Bias, variance & double descent

Why the classic U-curve is only half the story.

The art: 400 degree-9 polynomials, each fit to a fresh sample of 30 noisy points from a sine. The bright line is the truth. The spread you see is variance, made visible. (≈ 25 minutes for this section.)
The decomposition

Three sources of error, exactly

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(y-\hat f(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)]-f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\big[\hat f(x)\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}$$
The expectation is over training sets: imagine retraining on fresh data over and over. Bias = how far the average fit is from the truth. Variance = how much the refits wobble. σ² is the floor; no model beats it. Below: 200 resampled training sets of 25 points from sin(2πx) + noise. Degree 1 is consistently wrong (bias). Degree 12 is right on average but wild individually (variance). Question: which term does bagging attack? (Variance.) Which does boosting attack? (Mostly bias, at first.)
…then deep learning broke the U

Double descent

  • Error peaks where the model can just interpolate: p = n
  • Then it falls again: of all the perfect fits, min-norm picks the smoothest
  • A pinch of ridge erases the spike

Belkin et al., PNAS 2019 · Nakkiran et al., 2019. You'll reproduce this curve in Lab 1.1.

At p = n the system is square and badly conditioned: the only interpolating solution has a huge norm, so it amplifies noise. Past the threshold there are infinitely many interpolators; the pseudo-inverse picks the minimum-norm one, which gets smoother as p grows. That's implicit regularization. The ridge curves show explicit regularization flattens the spike entirely. Ask: where does early stopping in a neural net put you on this curve?
🍯 Key takeaway

"More parameters, more overfitting" is a rule for the classical regime only.

Past the interpolation threshold, implicit regularization from the optimizer, early stopping and the architecture decides which of the many perfect fits you get.

02

Gradient boosting, demystified

Gradient descent, where every step is a tree.

The art: all 400 stages of a from-scratch boosting run, drawn on top of each other. Purple is early (close to the constant mean), cream is late. You can watch the ensemble sweep from the mean toward the data, one small tree at a time.
The big idea

Descend in function space

  • Treat the predictions F(x₁), …, F(xₙ) as the parameters
  • The loss's negative gradient is the pseudo-residual r
  • A small tree approximates r; take a damped step ν

Friedman, Annals of Statistics 2001.

With n training points, the vector of predictions lives in Rⁿ; draw it for n = 2. The loss is a bowl centred on the targets y. The negative gradient at F₀ points at y (for squared loss it IS the residual). A tree can't represent that vector exactly (it's restricted to piecewise-constant functions), so we fit the closest tree and take a small step along it. Repeat. Ask: why not just step all the way to y? (That's memorizing the training set. The tree restriction plus shrinkage is what generalizes.)
The update

One tree per gradient step

$$r_i = -\frac{\partial L\big(y_i, F(x_i)\big)}{\partial F(x_i)}\bigg|_{F=F_{m-1}} \qquad h_m = \arg\min_{h \in \text{trees}} \sum_i \big(r_i - h(x_i)\big)^2 \qquad F_m = F_{m-1} + \nu\, h_m$$
Squared loss$r_i = y_i - F(x_i)$: the literal residual.
Log loss$r_i = y_i - p_i$: label minus predicted probability.
Shrinkage ν≈ 0.05–0.1. More trees, better generalization.
XGBoostA Newton step: leaf weight $w^* = -G/(H+\lambda)$.
Walk through left to right: gradient, fit, step. For log loss, F is the logit and the gradient of −log-likelihood w.r.t. the logit is p − y, so the pseudo-residual is y − p. XGBoost (2016) uses first and second derivatives (G and H summed over the leaf) for a Newton step and a regularized split gain. LightGBM (2017) added histogram binning and leaf-wise growth (watch num_leaves). CatBoost (2018) added ordered target statistics for categoricals and symmetric trees. scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting is a great first baseline with native missing-value support.
Small steps win

Shrink every step, then stop early

F = np.full(len(y), y.mean())
for m in range(M):
    r = y - F                 # -dL/dF
    h = Tree(max_depth=3).fit(X, r)
    F += lr * h.predict(X)    # small step

ν = 0.1 needs 30× more trees than ν = 1, and halves the validation error.

This loop is the whole algorithm (Lab 1.1 extends it to log loss, row subsampling and early stopping). Numbers from the figure: ν = 1 bottoms out at MSE 7.2 after 14 trees; ν = 0.1 reaches 3.6 after ~450 trees. Smaller steps keep each tree from over-committing to noise, so the ensemble averages more. In practice: set a small learning rate, set n_estimators high, and let early stopping on a validation set pick the number of trees. Ask: why does ν = 1 get worse after its minimum? (Each later tree fits noise in the residuals.)
🍯 Key takeaway

Boosting is gradient descent where every step is a tree.

Small learning rate, many trees, early stopping on held-out data. For any new table, a GBDT is the baseline to beat, and default feature importances are not to be trusted (use permutation importance or SHAP).

03

Uncertainty you can trust

Honest probabilities first, then guaranteed intervals.

Welcome back. The art: conformalized quantile regression bands at six coverage levels (40% to 98%) on data whose noise grows from left to right. Notice the bands widen where the data gets noisy. By the end of this block you'll know exactly why each band is guaranteed to cover what it claims.
Calibration

Is your 90% really 90%?

  • Accuracy can't see it. A reliability diagram can
  • Over-fit models (and many modern nets) are over-confident
  • Fix it on held-out data: Platt σ(a·s + b), isotonic, or temperature z / T
Calibrated means: among all the times the model says 70%, the event happens about 70% of the time. ECE bins predictions by confidence and averages |accuracy − confidence| weighted by bin size; it depends on the binning, so always look at the diagram too. Platt: 2 parameters, assumes a sigmoid-shaped distortion. Isotonic: any monotone map, flexible but needs more data. Temperature: 1 parameter, the default for neural nets (Guo et al., ICML 2017). Ask: where do you use a probability directly? Pricing, triage, thresholds, an LLM deciding whether to answer.
Quick check

Temperature scaling a network's logits changes…

its accuracy
its argmax predictions
its ranking of the classes
only its confidence, not its predictions

D. Dividing every logit by the same T > 0 preserves their order, so argmax, ranking and accuracy are untouched. Only the sharpness of the softmax changes.

Hands up for each option first, then reveal. Follow-up: so why can't temperature scaling fix a model whose ranking is wrong? (It can only stretch or squeeze confidence, not reorder.)
🍯 Key takeaway

A probability is a promise. Calibration is keeping it.

If a decision uses the number itself, check a reliability diagram, then fit Platt, isotonic or temperature scaling on held-out data, never on the training set.

Conformal prediction

A guarantee for any model

Wrap a GBM, a neural net, even an LLM. Assume only that calibration and test points are exchangeable.

$$\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{\text{new}} \in C(X_{\text{new}})\big) \ge 1-\alpha$$

Finite-sample, distribution-free. The +1 in ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉ is the test point itself.

The recipe: (1) hold out a calibration set the model never trained on; (2) compute nonconformity scores, here absolute residuals, and take the k-th smallest with k = ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉; (3) add ±q̂ around every prediction. Why it works: the new point's score is exchangeable with the n calibration scores, so its rank among all n+1 is uniform. The chance it lands above the k-th smallest is at most α. Five lines of numpy (the lab has them). The production library is MAPIE. 2026 frontier: conformal sets over LLM answers to decide when to abstain, conformal risk control, adaptive conformal for time series under shift.
Read the fine print

Guaranteed on average

  • One interval's coverage is random: exactly Beta(k, n+1−k)
  • Its mean, k/(n+1), is ≥ 1 − α for every n
  • With n = 20, about 1 run in 6 lands below 85%. With n = 500, almost never
This is the part people skip. The guarantee P(Y ∈ C) ≥ 1−α averages over the calibration draw AND the test point. Conditional on your particular calibration set, coverage follows Beta(k, n+1−k) (assuming continuous scores, no ties). Mean for n = 20 is 19/21 = 0.905; for n = 500, 451/501 = 0.900. Practical rule: use at least a few hundred calibration points if you care about the coverage of the one interval you deploy. Ask: what goes wrong if the calibration data comes from last year and the test data from today? (Exchangeability breaks.)
Pitfall

90% overall, 46% for the minority

  • The guarantee is marginal: averaged over all inputs
  • A noisy subgroup can be badly under-covered
  • Mondrian conformal calibrates per group: 90% each

CQR goes further and adapts the width to x (Lab 1.2).

Same model, same data. Marginal split conformal hits 90% overall by over-covering the quiet majority (98%) and under-covering the noisy minority (46%). Mondrian conformal just runs the recipe separately inside each group: group B's interval becomes ~4× wider, which is honest. Conditional coverage for every single x is impossible in general without assumptions, but group-conditional coverage is cheap. This is a fairness issue as much as a statistics one. Ask: what subgroups would you check in your own data?
🍯 Key takeaway

One quantile of held-out errors buys any model a coverage guarantee.

It assumes only exchangeability, and it holds on average. Calibrate on enough data, check coverage per subgroup, and watch for distribution shift.

04

Explain & dethrone

Shapley values, and the transformer coming for your GBDT.

The art: a SHAP beeswarm. Every dot is one patient's exact Shapley value for one feature (six features, all 64 coalitions enumerated), coloured by the feature's value. Rows are features, ordered by importance.
Explaining one prediction

Shapley values: fair credit, exactly

$$\phi_i = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,(|F|-|S|-1)!}{|F|!}\,\Big[v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S)\Big]$$
v(S)The model's output when only the features in S are "known".
The weightsAverage over every order in which features could join.
Efficiency$\sum_i \phi_i = f(x) - \mathbb{E}[f(X)]$, exactly.
The cost$2^{|F|}$ coalitions. TreeSHAP: polynomial, for trees.
From cooperative game theory (Shapley, 1953): the unique attribution satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy and additivity. SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, NeurIPS 2017) applies it to model predictions. The crucial, often-hidden choice is v(S): "interventional" fills the missing features from a background dataset; "observational" conditions on the known features. They can disagree sharply with correlated features. In Lab 1.3 you brute-force it for 4 features and check TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al., 2020) matches.
Computed by brute force

Why this patient scores 253

  • 6 features → all 64 coalitions, no approximation
  • The bars sum exactly to f(x) − E[f(X)]
  • Absent features are filled from 120 background patients
Read it bottom to top: start at the average prediction (149), add each feature's contribution, land on this patient's prediction (253). High triglycerides and blood pressure explain most of the gap; being 36 pulls it down a little. The script asserts that the contributions sum to f(x) − E[f(X)] to 1e-6. Ask: does this tell us lowering triglycerides would lower this patient's progression? (No: it describes the model, not the body. That's the next slide.)
Pitfall

Duplicate a feature, the credit splits

  • Add a near-copy of x₁ (ρ ≈ 0.999)
  • Total credit for the pair stays put
  • The split depends on the random seed

SHAP explains the model, not the world. Always say which value function you used.

Same data-generating process every time; only the random seed of the GBDT changes. The trees happen to split on x₁ or on its copy, and SHAP faithfully reports what this particular model used, anywhere from about 70/30 to 85/15. So "feature A matters more than feature B" is fragile whenever A and B are correlated. The same problem hits default gain importances, which also favour high-cardinality features. Remedies: group correlated features and attribute to the group, or use permutation importance on held-out data with care.
Frontier · Nature 2025

TabPFN: learning in context

🧠 Shines: ≲ 10k rows, no tuning time, calibrated probabilities out of the box.
🌳 GBDTs still rule: millions of rows, tight CPU latency, heavy feature engineering.
TabPFN (Hollmann et al., Nature 2025) is pretrained on millions of synthetic datasets drawn from a prior over structural causal models. At inference it reads your whole labelled training set plus the test rows as one input and predicts the test labels in context, exactly like an LLM learning from examples in its prompt. Because it's trained to predict held-out labels across a huge family of problems, it approximates Bayesian posterior prediction under that prior, which is why its probabilities tend to be well calibrated. In Lab 1.3 you run a fair head-to-head and decide for yourself. Ask: what's the "training" cost of TabPFN on your data? (Zero gradient steps, but the forward pass scales with the size of the context.)
🍯 Key takeaway

SHAP explains the model, not the world. And the model may soon be pretrained.

State your value function and beware correlated features. On small tables, give an in-context learner like TabPFN a fair shot against your tuned GBDT.
